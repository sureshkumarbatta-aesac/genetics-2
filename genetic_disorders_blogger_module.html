1. What is a genetic disorder?

A genetic disorder is a condition associated with a change in genetic material that affects normal body function. Changes may involve a single gene, several genes or chromosomes. Some are inherited; others arise from new genetic changes.

Gene disorders

Changes in one gene

Examples include sickle-cell disease, many forms of thalassaemia, cystic fibrosis and phenylketonuria.

Chromosomal disorders

Changes in chromosome number or structure

Examples include Down syndrome (trisomy 21) and Turner syndrome (45,X).

Remember: The six conditions in this lesson are primarily single-gene disorders. Chromosomal disorders form a separate category.

2. Explore the six disorders

Use each card to revise the cause, inheritance and key exam facts.

01 • Blood clotting

Haemophilia

Cause: Deficiency or dysfunction of a clotting factor.

Inheritance: Haemophilia A and B are usually X-linked recessive. Haemophilia C is generally autosomal.

Exam facts: A = factor VIII; B = factor IX; C = factor XI. Symptoms may include prolonged bleeding, bruising and bleeding into joints.

02 • Red blood cells

Sickle-cell disease

Cause: A variant in the HBB gene on chromosome 11 produces haemoglobin S.

Inheritance: Autosomal recessive.

Exam facts: Glutamic acid is replaced by valine at position 6 of the mature beta-globin chain. Red cells may become sickle-shaped, causing anaemia, pain and blocked blood vessels.

03 • Metabolism

Phenylketonuria (PKU)

Cause: Usually deficiency of phenylalanine hydroxylase due to variants in the PAH gene on chromosome 12.

Inheritance: Autosomal recessive.

Exam facts: Phenylalanine accumulates if untreated and can harm brain development. Newborn screening and appropriate dietary management are important.

04 • Vision

Colour vision deficiency

Cause: Changes in cone photopigments or cone function affect colour discrimination.

Inheritance: Common red-green forms are usually X-linked recessive; other types can have different genetic causes.

Exam facts: Protan affects red perception; deutan affects green perception; tritan affects blue-yellow discrimination. Ishihara plates screen mainly for red-green deficiency.

05 • Haemoglobin

Thalassaemia

Cause: Reduced or absent production of alpha- or beta-globin chains.

Inheritance: Usually autosomal recessive.

Exam facts: Alpha-globin genes are on chromosome 16; the beta-globin gene cluster is on chromosome 11. Severity ranges from mild to severe anaemia.

06 • Cell transport

Cystic fibrosis

Cause: Variants in the CFTR gene on chromosome 7 affect chloride and bicarbonate transport.

Inheritance: Autosomal recessive.

Exam facts: Thick, sticky secretions can affect the lungs and pancreas. Elevated sweat chloride is an important diagnostic finding.

6. Board-exam model answer (8 marks)

Common genetic disorders: Genetic disorders are conditions associated with changes in genes or chromosomes. Single-gene disorders may follow autosomal or sex-linked inheritance. Examples include:

  1. Haemophilia: A bleeding disorder caused by deficiency or dysfunction of clotting factors. Haemophilia A involves factor VIII and B involves factor IX; both are usually X-linked recessive.
  2. Sickle-cell disease: An autosomal recessive disorder associated with an HBB variant on chromosome 11. Glutamic acid is replaced by valine in the beta-globin chain, and red cells may become sickle-shaped.
  3. Phenylketonuria: Usually an autosomal recessive metabolic disorder caused by phenylalanine hydroxylase deficiency. Phenylalanine accumulation can damage brain development if untreated.
  4. Colour vision deficiency: Altered cone photopigments affect colour discrimination. Common red-green forms are usually X-linked recessive.
  5. Thalassaemia: Reduced production of alpha- or beta-globin chains can damage red blood cells and cause anaemia.
  6. Cystic fibrosis: An autosomal recessive disorder caused by CFTR gene variants on chromosome 7. Thick secretions can affect the lungs and pancreas.

Conclusion: Knowledge of genetic disorders helps explain inheritance and supports early diagnosis, appropriate care and genetic counselling.

Exam tip: If a question asks for both Mendelian and chromosomal disorders, include chromosomal examples such as Down syndrome (trisomy 21) and Turner syndrome (45,X). They are not single-gene conditions.
